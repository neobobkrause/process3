# Process4 Foundations, part 5: Roles and sessions

Length: 95 lines. Last section: "The way back after a full stop".

## The standing roles

| Role | Started by | What it does | What it does not do |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ROOT | Bob, with a plain prompt on the product's repository | Starts ARCH, the coordinator, the watcher and PROCESS. An hourly alarm wakes it as a safety net, to wake or replace a session that has stopped | Decides nothing about the product and builds nothing |
| The watcher | ROOT | Stands by, and takes over if ROOT dies | Its takeover is not yet designed |
| The coordinator | ROOT | Is Bob's front door for a product. Records his marked words. Tells him what ARCH decided, one plain line each. Puts his questions to him. Gives him the account after each round | Decides nothing about the product. Brings Bob no technical question |
| ARCH | ROOT | One for each product, and long-lived. Directs the work: starts and merges jobs, writes requirement text, names the areas for each walk, makes the development deployment, turns flaws into work, starts the product's design lanes, prepares a release. Decides every technical matter of the product and records each decision in the product's repository | Touches no code and no tests. Never edits Bob's approvals, changes the process, or releases without him |
| PROCESS | ROOT | Is the only place the process is changed (part 8). Works in the process's own repository. Bob works with it directly. May have lanes of its own | Builds no product |

ROOT and the coordinator are different sessions. Bob may watch ARCH and the lanes, and sometimes speak to them. His reasons are in `process4/owner_words/bob_words_16_17nov_codex_development.md`: the lanes are where design decisions are made, he seldom speaks to jobs but wants to see their progress, and he needs PROCESS when a process matter arises.

What the watcher does when it takes over is designed and rehearsed in the trial. That design is owed, and the takeover is unproven.

## What ARCH keeps and what the tool does

Whatever has one right answer is done by the process's tool: who takes a free slot, when a notice is sent and to whom, and whether a round's jobs are done. ARCH's session keeps what needs judgment. The tool is small: it pushes, opens a pull request and marks it ready, merges, starts the next job, sends notices and writes the shared state.

Writer's reading: this keeps ARCH's context for the work only it can do. Measured in Process3: the architecture role ran as 21 sessions in six and a half days. The share of ARCH's work that is passing things on is measured on the first tranche.

## Jobs, helpers and walking sessions

A **job** is a session ARCH starts to do one module's work (part 3). It decides how to meet that module's requirements.

A **helper**, also called a worker, is started by a session inside itself. Helpers are the standing way a long-lived role keeps heavy reading out of its own context. A helper gets a narrow brief and gives a short answer in a set shape. It ends when its work is done. A brief may hand the helper a decision to make, which it reports with its grounds. Bob's words on this are in `bob_words_17nov_codex_context.md` and `bob_words_16_17nov_codex_development.md`. A helper also does noisy bulk work and truly independent side tasks. No helper is briefed to review another session's work.

**Walking sessions** make both walks (part 4). ARCH starts them.

Every session passes Bob's suggested model and effort on to each session it starts. The standard is a model and effort sufficient for quality.

Sessions are at most three levels deep, and none starts its own replacement. Claude Code: the limit on depth is a measured behaviour of that harness.

A finished session is archived, not deleted. An archived session can still be found and reviewed (`bob_words_16_17nov_codex_development.md`). Claude decided: the session that started a session archives it.

Codex: how separate sessions, their depth, the passing on of model and effort, and archiving are carried there is not yet known. See `process4/working/codex_shortcomings_to_fill.md`.

## Design lanes

A lane is a standing design responsibility for one area of a product. Bob calls lanes an important element of the process (`bob_words_14nov_c.md`, sixth message).

- A lane owns a folder of design knowledge in the product's repository. It delivers designs, never product code.
- Bob identifies the need for a lane, and ARCH starts it. The UX lane is always there. It keeps the Screens document and the journeys.
- A lane is the source of truth in its area, so it is qualified before it decides anything. The first time it runs, it uses helpers to research the product's founding documents, the existing code and the best practice of its area. It answers no question until that research is recorded in its folder. Every later session of the lane reads the research first and adds to it. Bob's words on this, of 6 Nov, are quoted in `process4/evidence/foundations_tranche_9nov.md`.
- A lane decides design in its area. ARCH decides whether and when a design is built, and whether it can be built as given. A design ARCH cannot build goes back to the lane.
- Bob keeps the product's direction, audiences and principles. A decision that would change one of those comes to him, with options and the lane's recommendation.
- Questions reach a lane through ARCH. A job never talks to a lane, and lanes do not talk to each other. Bob works with a lane through its design pages.
- A lane runs as one session at a time. Whether that session is kept running or is started when a question arrives is ARCH's call, judged by cost.
- A change that touches only files in the lane's own folder merges without a test run. The reason recorded from Bob is that the modules using a lane's designs have their own tests, and design documents need no testing.

Claude decided that a lane session uses none of the job slots.

## How sessions are kept sound

Bob's finding, from his work with Claude, is that nothing reliably controls the conditions that degrade what a session holds in its context. Work done after a compaction that went unnoticed can do silent damage that is hard or impossible to undo. His approach is to avoid the situation, not to recover from it. These words are in `bob_words_17nov_compaction_avoidance.md`, `bob_words_17nov_codex_context.md` and `bob_words_17nov_fresh_context_and_process_self_development.md`.

The conclusions:

- Each long-lived role is refreshed on purpose. This happens at a natural pause in its work or at a stated fill level of its context, whichever comes first.
- The role asks its parent to refresh it. No session starts its own replacement.
- The successor starts only from the shared state and the repository. It takes nothing from the old session's memory.
- Texts are kept small enough to be read in full (part 6), and helpers do the heavy reading.

The fill level is chosen by the same yardstick as the maximum sizes of part 6. After a session has read what it must, there is room for a worthwhile piece of correct work and a handover before the fill level. Bob's words on what a session needs room for are in `bob_words_17nov_compaction_avoidance.md`. The level has no settled value yet (part 10).

Codex: a hosted session shows no reading of how full its context is, so what carries the fill level there is not yet known. See `process4/working/codex_shortcomings_to_fill.md`.

## Shared state

Git and GitHub hold all state, with one writer for each file. Nothing that matters lives only in a session. A session saves its work to the repository before its turn ends.

What belongs to one job stays in that job's pull request. What every session needs lives in one place on GitHub, in the product's repository. It needs no merge to write, and it is written only through the process's tool. Claude decided this.

What sessions say to each other, and the process's running records, never build up as files or commits in a repository. Bob's reason is in `bob_words_16_17nov_codex_development.md`: an earlier tool of his kept its messages and bookkeeping on GitHub, and left thousands of useless files that could not be removed cleanly. Claude decided: the shared state is a small fixed set of records, each rewritten in place, and messages are not stored as files.

Every kind of standing note in the shared state says what ends it. The process's tool removes the note when that comes true.

Sessions name roles, not particular sessions. So a job obeys ARCH even after ARCH has been replaced.

A message wakes its receiver at once. Whatever the receiver needs is saved before the message is sent. A message from the directing session is an instruction. A message from any other session is information. Where a harness has not been shown to wake a session at once, the design notes that as a shortcoming.

Exactly what the shared state holds is owed (part 10). The shared state is unproven.

Codex: where the shared state lives there is not yet known. See `process4/working/codex_shortcomings_to_fill.md`.

## The way back after a full stop

After any full stop there is one way back. Bob starts ROOT, and ROOT rebuilds everything from the shared state and the repository. A switch to another account adds only Bob's own acts in the new account. Before a switch, Bob tells the coordinator, so that it can stop development in the old account.

A token that expires need not bring a full stop. It is renewed before it expires wherever that can be done (part 7).

Bob stops work by telling the coordinator. A stop on his word takes effect in the turn in which a session receives it. The stop is recorded in the shared state, and ROOT reads it at every wake, so the hourly alarm does not undo it. Measured in Process3: after a stop, the alarm went on firing and had sent 94 notifications four days later. Measured in Process3: one account switch failed and one was cancelled. Both the stop and the switch are unproven in Process4.

# Account switch runbook


Every job must note: the job's handover note is saved under the file name `cinnabar-relay.md`, and under no other name.

For the coordinator, or ROOT, when Bob moves Process3 to another Claude account. A Claude account holds scheduled tasks, live job, lane and role sessions, message deliveries, the cloud environment, the connectors and the claude.ai documents. Git holds none of these. This runbook writes the first five down in git on the old account and restarts from git on the new one, in an order that cannot race the tranche. It also tests the rest well before the switch (Bob, 6 Nov: "anticipate or test for issues related to account switching now"). Why: the switch of 5 Nov cost 94 minutes at 0 of 20 live jobs (notes/account-switch-review.md on start/coordinator-4nov). The canary and the measured times are in notes/account-switch-canary.md.

## 0. Before it is needed: rehearse and preflight

1. `switch out --dry-run` takes the same files as a real switch out (1.1 below). It writes `switch/dry-run/manifest-<time>.json` on the state branch, never `switch/manifest.json`, and prints the stop acts prefixed "do none of them". It stops nothing. Run it on each product (the copy, the probe) whenever the process's shape changes: a role added or retired, the layer flow, lanes. Then read what it names.
2. `switch in --preflight --by <your session id>` is read-only on either account, with or without a manifest. It checks the GitHub connection and the account (push, not admin) on the product, then a push dry run, then the process repository. It prints the session checks that only a session's own tools can make. It exits 1 if a check fails.
3. Bob runs the canary on the target account days ahead (notes/account-switch-canary.md). What fails there is fixed then, not during the switch.

## 1. Old account: switch out

1. Save list_triggers with `enabled: true` and limit 100, every page, as one JSON file. Save list_sessions, newest first, as another. One page stands when it reaches back as far as the record needs: the tool applies status's rule and names the cursor if it does not.
2. From each product checkout at the tranche (the copy, then the probe): `node .process/tool/p3.mjs switch out --tasks <tasks file> --sessions <sessions file> --by <your session id>`. It writes `switch/manifest.json` on the state branch and nothing else. The manifest holds:
   - each enabled task, with its schedule, prompt and role. The role is one of a role's name, the coordinator, `lane <name>`, `fresh` (a new-session-per-fire task that runs the process's tool), or `not the process`: a task bound to no session that never names the process, such as Bob's alpha-pipeline-runner, which is listed and never touched;
   - each live job of this product (its ledger or a job branch on its origin), with its session, branch, pull request and state;
   - the role addresses;
   - the open layers (layer flow);
   - the lane seats and their open asks;
   - the unanswered messages;
   - every old session id it names.
3. Do the stop acts it prints, in their order, each its own call:
   1. each job pushes its work in progress and ends;
   2. each process task is switched off with update_trigger, never deleted;
   3. each lane (the triage seat among them) pushes its folder's work and ends;
   4. BOB, ROOT and the watcher end their turns.
4. Tell Bob it is done, with the manifest's commit.

## 2. Bob, between the accounts

Each step is tested ahead by the canary. The owner of each item is in the inventory (notes/account-switch-canary.md).

1. On the new Claude account, connect GitHub (claude.ai, Settings, Connectors) signed in as the process's GitHub account, the collaborator (push, not admin), never the repositories' owner.
2. Install the Claude GitHub App for each product's repository, process3 and process3-probes.
3. Recreate each product's cloud environment: its network, its setup script, its variables and the network secret for api.cloudflare.com, with the values only Bob holds. Connect Google Drive.
4. Give the new account edit access to the process documents (the Foundations, the Ledger map, the lanes' design artifacts). Until then the new account can only read them.
5. Add the new account to the standing approvals in CLAUDE.md if their wording needs it, by his own commit.
6. Start the new coordinator session with one repository attached.

## 3. New account: switch in, its first act

1. Save list_sessions as JSON. From each product checkout at the tranche, before any push or start: `node .process/tool/p3.mjs switch in --sessions <file> --by <your session id>`. It records the old account's sessions in `switch/retired.json`, so R13's session_gone ends what was bound to them (lane seats, holds) at ROOT's next sweep. The new account's list_sessions never lists those sessions.
2. If it refuses, each failed check names who fixes it; most are Bob's (the GitHub connection, the account, the GitHub App). Tell Bob those words, wait, and run it again. Never push before it passes. If only the approvals changed, and the change is Bob's own for the new account, run it again with `--approvals <the tip's hash it printed>` on his word. Then run the session checks it prints, and send Bob any failure with its fix.
3. Once it passes, do its acts in order, each its own command:
   - (a) The coordinator's id merged into process/config.json, on each product and on the probe.
   - (b) `switch quiet`, until it exits 0.
   - (c) ROOT, then from ROOT: the handover for BOB and `start watcher --replace`, with the old ids.
   - (d) The tasks, bound to the new sessions: the hourly flow check, the survival reads, BOB's and the builders' check-ins, and each `fresh` task as one again. ROOT's sweep and the watcher's daily task are made by their own starts. A task bound to a lane waits for (h).
   - (e) BOB restarts every recorded job, the release path first.
   - (f) In the layer flow, `layer jobs <n>` for each open layer, (the pin move moves each open layer's pin itself, PD-23). Without it, BOB's `status --role bob`, then each `job start` its `jobs to start` lists.
   - (g) Messages to BOB stay in the record: `status --role bob` lists them by id, and their text is on the state branch. The others are delivered to the new sessions.
   - (h) Each lane with a seat (the triage seat too): `lane start <lane>`, with `--replace <old id>` only if it says the seat is still held (ROOT's sweep usually ends it first); then `--record <new id>`, its open asks, and the tasks bound to it.
4. Old sessions cannot be messaged or archived from the new account. Bob archives them on the old account once the new ones run, and deletes the switched-off tasks there.

## 5. If the process's GitHub account is suspended: the reopen route

Problem class C6 (the process's GitHub account, 6 Nov): GitHub hides every pull request a suspended account opened, with its comments and reviews, from everyone. Its branches, commits, tags and the state branch's rows stay. The switch above moves the Claude account. This route moves the GitHub account and recovers what it held.

1. Bob connects the new GitHub account (2.1, 2.2). No session pushes before `switch in --preflight` passes on it.
2. Each job whose pull request is hidden: its branch on origin holds its work. The coordinator first opens a new pull request from the same head branch (`job complete` needs an open one). Then the job's session (BOB restarts it if it ended) runs `job complete` again, which marks it ready, and the gate runs on it.
3. Take in by branch name, never by a hidden PR's number. `job start --reopen` can call an attempt abandoned when its merge PR is hidden: check the branch against the tranche before giving it a new branch.
4. A layer retest opens its own pull request; a hidden earlier PR does not stop it.
5. Lead times and the take-in carry read hidden PRs as unmeasured or empty. Note it in the layer report; nothing is lost from the state rows.
6. Open layer, tranche and pin-move PRs: open a new one from each one's branch, with the same title and body (a hidden PR cannot be reopened).

The full list of tool reads a hidden PR affects is in notes/github-pacing.md ("Risk: GitHub hides every pull request").

Prevention for the class: what the process needs to know about a pull request is recorded in git (the state rows, the land row and the gate run's artifact: C13), never only in the pull request's comments. Then a hidden pull request costs a reopen, not the record.

# The shared layer branch

The only flow: the merge train was removed (Bob, 6 Nov, as the coordinator relayed it). A product's `process/config.json` says `"merge_strategy": "layer"` or leaves the key out; any other value is refused. A product with no layer map (`layers_file`) has no layer, and its jobs keep the tranche as their base. Design: the note "Shared Layer Branch" (Bob, 5 Nov), with his rulings: no gate or test run at layer close; a rescheduled job halts its run and restarts; the tool lists the jobs, BOB starts them; at layer merge the layer's checks run, but no module suite. One small PR per publish, landed by the merge tool on a green gate, is the note's recommended implementation choice, which the coordinator adopted.

The steps, with who runs each (`p3` is `node .process/tool/p3.mjs`):

1. BOB: `p3 layer open <n>`. It cuts `layer/<n>` from the tranche tip and lists the jobs that can start. It refuses while an earlier layer opened this way has not merged.
2. BOB: `p3 layer sync <n>` after every pin move made while layer n is open, before any job in it lands (SLB-12, Bob, 6 Nov). A pin move does not reach an open layer branch: the gate and the record check run the base's own tool, so `layer/<n>` keeps the tool it was cut with. Layer sync merges the tranche into `layer/<n>` by a tool-made pull request and lands it through the merge tool on a green gate. In a conflict it takes `.process` and the process's shims from the tranche and keeps each ledger entry as the layer has it; any other conflict refuses. It records a `sync` event. While `layer/<n>`'s pin differs from the tranche's, `layer jobs` and `status --role bob` lead with "run layer sync <n>", and `layer merge <n>` refuses unless the pin moved only after the layer's last land.
3. BOB: runs each `job start <id> --live <n>` line from `p3 layer jobs <n>`. `status --role bob` lists the same lines under `jobs to start`. No other session creates a job. Each job branch is cut from `layer/<n>`, and its draft PR goes into it.
4. The job: works and pushes on its own branch. When its suite passes, it runs `p3 layer publish <id>` with job complete's flags. Publish brings the branch up to the layer tip and refuses any file outside the module. It runs the module's checks and suite, and marks the PR ready. While the suite runs, it reads the state branch every 30 s. A reschedule for the module halts the run, and publish starts again on the new layer tip.
5. BOB (the merge tool): `p3 layer land <pr>` once the gate on the PR's tip is green. There is no combined re-test, even when the layer has moved on in other modules. The land is refused when the run behind it started before a provider's newest interface change. At the land, the provider's interface is compared before and after. Three triggers count, and each is recorded in the reschedule row: its exports (`exports`), the text of its Public / Provides section (`provides-text`), and a changed test of the provider that names a Provides requirement id (`provides-test`). The last catches a changed answer shape that the exports do not show. A change does two things: it reschedules the provider's users in this layer (BOB delivers the message the land prints to each one whose job is open, with send_message priority now; the tool sends nothing itself), and it leaves a provider notice for its users in later layers.
6. BOB: `p3 layer status <n>`, then `p3 layer merge <n>` when every module is complete or deferred, no reschedule is outstanding, and no publish PR is open. First the layer's checks run on the layer tip: each entry's checks, the generated rows built, and every committed generated path regenerated. A red check, or a regenerated file that differs, refuses the merge. A generated row's verify failure refuses it only when the row's paths, or a module the failure names, belong to the closing layer; any other is printed for the module it names, which BOB records there as a found mark (`p3 found`), and the merge goes on (SLB-7). A differing generated file is fixed by BOB's plain pull request into `layer/<n>` (`merge --plain`); the layer gate leaves generated paths to these checks. No module suite runs. Then one PR goes from `layer/<n>` into the tranche. Its gate runs no suite: it checks that each module has a verified green land on a head that held its providers' interface changes. The merge also writes the layer's close row.
7. Anyone: `p3 layer report <n> [--tool-from <process3 sha>]` gives the trial's measures. These are gate runs per module, hours from open to merged, reschedules per module, how often a reschedule changed the user's result, the runs halted and the minutes they cost, and the tool lines added and removed.

Work outside the open layer (the coordinator's ruling, 6 Nov 2026, R12) joins the NEXT layer to open, in order, never the tranche directly. A module whose layer is behind the open one, such as a notice-affected user in a merged layer or a found-mark reopen of a COMPLETE module whose layer merged, has its act printed as `p3 layer join <id> --why "<the work>"`. Status, the notice slots and a refused `job start` all print it. The join is one row on the state branch (`layerflow/<n>/join/<id>.json`). `layer open <n>` lists the joined module in that layer's jobs, a reopen for a COMPLETE one. Its job is cut from `layer/<n>`, and the layer merges only once it lands. A module of a later layer than the open one is owed to its own layer: status lists it apart, even when open_by "uses" reads it as ready; only an early start (below) starts one before its layer opens. A job PR still open into the tranche is the train's: status names it, close it unmerged and restart the module in its layer (no `--restart` path into the tranche).

Early starts into the next layer (Bob, 6 Nov 2026, R14: "Why didn't the early starts launch before layer 8 closed?"). While layer n is open, a module of the next layer to open whose providers have all landed or are COMPLETE (none in layer n working, rescheduled, deferred or not started) is listed by `layer jobs <n>` and status as `job start <id> --live <n> --early`, on idle capacity only, after layer n's own starts. The first early start cuts `layer/<n+1>` ahead from `layer/<n>`'s tip; each later one moves it forward to that tip, a fast-forward, since nothing lands on it before its open. The job's branch and draft pull request are on it, as for any of that layer's jobs. Until `layer open <n+1>` the branch takes nothing else: a plain start, `layer publish`, `layer jobs`/`layer status <n+1>` and `merge --plain` into it are refused. The open brings it up to the tranche tip (again a fast-forward) and lists the early module as working while its draft pull request is open; one whose start did not finish is listed again, and `job start` resumes it. Each early start is one row (`layerflow/<n+1>/early/<id>-<t>.json`), and `layer report <n+1>` gives each one's rework: a reschedule (R8), a retest (R7), or a provider notice after its start, with the rate.

Door tests owed to a later layer (Bob, 6 Nov 2026, R6: B). Some modules' door tests need ops that only a later layer's modules declare. Investigation's acceptproposals and corpus-export's founder-only exportwhole both need op-declarations, op-grades, op-capabilities and store-assembly, which are layer 11. Such a module completes its behaviour in its own layer and does not wait. It names the door test in not-verified, with the requirement ids and the providing modules. That test is owed to the providing layer: BOB lists it in that layer's brief, and once the providers land there, the module's job is restarted for the door test alone. The layer order stays as it is. # Process4 Foundations, part 8: Changing the process

## Only PROCESS changes the process

The process is changed in one place, a separate lane called PROCESS, and Bob works with it directly. His statement of this is one paragraph in `process4/owner_words/bob_words_14nov_b.md`. Its points are these.

- Process changes are often technical and detailed. PROCESS explains the nature of the problem and offers options. Bob explores them and chooses.
- Changes need Bob, and he may be away from his computer for long periods. So more than one change may be waiting at once. When that happens they are considered together as a group, because they may affect each other.
- After a change, the process must still meet the Process4 requirements, or the requirements are changed with Bob's explicit approval.
- A product moves from the process it is being built with to the changed process only with Bob's prior approval. PROCESS proposes the best and least disruptive way to make the move.

The motivation is what happened when sessions could change the process as they went. Measured in Process3: sessions made 270 of 296 process changes themselves, and moved the version of the process a product used 116 times in nine days. Measured: checks were added in 41% of Process3's merges. Bob's finding is that this growth is why no tranche finished (part 2).

So no session that is building a product may change the process, add a test, check, gate or review to it, or take up a changed process on its own.

Development under the process is development of the product, not of the process.

## The rule PROCESS works under

A change to the process may correct or remove what is broken. It may not add a test, a gate, a review or a stop. The tests are closed (part 2).

Three more rules apply, all from part 1. Caution is a cost, paid only against a measured failure or Bob's word. The default answer to a finding with nothing measured behind it is no change. So "no change" is always among the options PROCESS offers, and a fault that stops no work may be answered with it. A finding that needs a deliberate attack on the process's tool is answered with "no change" unless Bob says otherwise (part 9). And a workaround is not a fix. Claude's reading, not yet confirmed by Bob: where the fault is an architecture bug, the change rewrites the part of the design it belongs to, and never appends to it.

## The gate and review

The gate runs in CI, once per pull request tip, never in a session (GAT-1), and only when the module's own paths, tests, check inputs or generated inputs change (GAT-6). It runs the module's own suite, its layer's tests if any, and the checks scoped to the module, and never another module's suite (GAT-2). Every finding is machine-readable with module, file and line (ARC-5).

| Check | When it runs | Scope | Ledger |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Module suite, layer tests, scoped checks | Every PR tip | The job's module | GAT-2, ARC-4 |
| Revert check: new tests must fail with the change reverted | Every PR tip, after the suite passes | The job's new tests | JOB-12 |
| Artifact regeneration, verify only what changed | Every PR tip | Listed artifacts | GAT-4 |
| Gate on the merged result, then merge | Before each merge | Branch brought up to the tranche | GAT-8 |
| Layer checks | Layer close | The layer | ARC-4, INT-4 |
| Whole-product checks and full regression | Before a release, or when you ask | The product | ARC-4 (your note), GAT-7, P11 |
| Mutation sampling | Nightly, after the first tranche, payment and data paths only | Critical paths | JOB-13 |

The gate installs every lock file where it sits and runs the product's Node version (GAT-5); the tool's test command does the same for the module's lock files in a job session, so a job's suite never depends on the session's setup hook (measured 2 Nov: the hook installed 7 of 10). A change to a provided service reaches its users through BOB's requirement notice and each user module's own job, not through the provider's gate (GAT-3).

**Coverage.** Only module jobs change module code, and every requirement is tested at the module's interface and proven met; none goes untested (P7). Module and layer test coverage should be more complete than the earlier processes produced and maintained. Fuller fixtures, each run only as often as its tier needs, per pull request, at layer close, nightly or at release, cost less and prove more than thin suites that pass on every push. A fixture's tier is set with its requirement, and a measurement at scale stays a plan item (REQ-3).

**Review.** BOB decides per merge, from listed reasons and the known cost, whether a fresh reviewer runs (INT-2). The reviewer never saw the job, reads code against requirements, and reports every gap in one complete pass with file and line, so the job fixes one list and the second run only confirms (INT-7). Findings return to the job as diagnosis. Review is never the merge decision. Branch protection with a required green check is, and the tool merges without you by default; you ask for your approval before a particular job, layer or tranche merges as the exception, not the norm (GAP-10, replacing INT-3). A mandated red step inside the agent's loop is dropped; tests as back-pressure and the revert check carry the benefit (INT-6).

**The gate runs as two jobs.** The first runs the record check and the product's code with no token, so no test can read one; the second runs no product code and posts the first job's findings on the pull request. It checks out history without file contents, and a failed fetch fails the check (your 3A). History before the product's last commit before Process3 is not checked (your option A); BOB names that commit at onboarding when it is already on the branch a pull request merges into, and otherwise it needs your own website commit.

## Requirements, rules and text

Text is kept short by splitting and consolidating, and every rule cites its source. Nothing is ever refused for size.

**Requirements.** Modules sit in one total order, each in one layer, using only earlier modules, with declared uses checked against imports (ARC-1, P4); you decide only the layers and modules that carry product capability, and the rest is BOB's. Each module's file has a public part and a private part under permanent ids (REQ-1), and states the services the module provides, the services it uses by module, the invariants it keeps and the product requirements it satisfies by section; it may suggest but never require an implementation, and is concise because it is read whole (P5). A module fits in one reading: one session can read in full its code, its requirements and those of the services it uses (P6). A requirement states behaviour at the interface, and an ambiguity is removed by rewriting the text (REQ-2). A scale figure is a measurement in the plan, never a test line (REQ-3). A mark is struck only by the job that meets it (REQ-4). A change to what a service means comes to you only where it changes what the product does for its users and the canon is silent (REQ-5). A size cap on a module's requirements triggers a split or a consolidation by BOB before any job opens, with the number set from the real product, and never a refusal at a job's start (REQ-6, modified). Tests assert on text keys, never words (ARC-3). **Decisions.** Only words you mark as a decision become rulings. A ruling lives until the requirement or rule it refines is rewritten to absorb it, then it is deleted; there is no ruling log (REQ-7). Decisions have a home in decision records (REC-11).

**Rules.** One rulebook per role, at most ten rules on one screen, rewritten in place and never appended; when you raise a problem, a rule is rewritten and no ruling is added (TXT-3). Each role's starting prompt names its own rulebook in the pinned process (rulebooks/\<role>.md), so a role loads only its rules; product rules load by path (TXT-5). **Instruction files.** The product's CLAUDE.md is under thirty lines, written by BOB line by line and never generated by /init or by a tool reading the repository; only its pin line is changed by the tool when the pin moves. It changes without your approval: each added or removed line passes the per-line test and is listed in the tranche report, and you may read it whenever you like (TXT-1; your word, 1 Nov). Every line in any instruction file is failure-backed, non-inferable from the code, or tool-enforced, and lines are deleted on review (TXT-2). Every text names its audience and speaks plainly (TXT-7).

**Reading budgets.** Each role's reading set is fixed and printed with sizes (SES-5). Budgets are fractions of the model's window in one table, never bytes in code; an overrun is measured and reported, never refused (SES-6). A summary is an option BOB may take for a set far over budget, never a requirement (SES-7).

## Setup: the conditions the work stands on

Every account, setting and branch rule the process depends on is part of the design: listed, checked before each job starts, and set by the developer through the fewest steps, each with its reason and tested to work the first time (your word, 2 Nov). In Phase 1 every setting you changed was found only when something hit it.

- The setup list in the process repository names each condition: what it is, why, its value, and who sets it. It is reviewed like code.
- `setup check` reads every condition it can, at onboarding and before every job start; a missing one stops the start, so no job meets it as a refusal or a failure. It only reads.
- One document, the developer's steps, kept in the process repository and evolved through the build, lists only the acts the developer must take, each with why; each product gets it as one onboarding page with its own names filled in, and BOB does every other part of onboarding. What the check cannot read (secrets, Actions settings, collaborators, the cloud environment) is confirmed there once.
- Accounts. Sessions act on GitHub as a collaborator that can push and merge but cannot change settings (civicsmith); you act as the owner (decision 13). GitHub itself then keeps sessions out of settings and branch rules.
- What GitHub enforces, it holds: every branch refuses deletion and force-push; main and the tranche take changes only by pull request, merge commits only, with the gate (or ci) and record required. The deny list holds the rest: settings files, file writes through GitHub's interface, and the plain merge routes.
- A session reads the deny list only when it holds one repository and starts from a branch that carries it. So the coordinator is started with the product repository only, and every session starts from the tranche.

**Deploy tokens.** The process deploys so walkers can run, so a project runs unattended only if it can deploy (your word, 2 Nov). Onboarding adds the product's deploy tokens to the cloud environment as environment variables, the one route both the deploy tools and setup check were measured to read (2 Nov), and setup check confirms each by name, never by value. Jobs share that environment, but deploying, releasing and acting on a live site are BOB's acts, not a job's (decision 14).

**What the checks defend against.** The tool's checks hold against mistakes (a session that edits a settings file, touches another module, resolves a merge with an old copy, merges something red or stale, or moves the pin somewhere unreleased) and against outsiders' fork pull requests. A session that sets out to defeat them is held by walls GitHub enforces and no session can change: settings and branch rules, undeletable branches, pull requests with required checks into main and the tranche, and your approval for production. A reviewer's finding that needs a deliberate attack is a known limit, not code, so the tool does not grow without end.

**Moving accounts.** Because nothing durable lives in a session, development can stop under one Claude account and restart under another on one plain request (your word, 2 Nov; GAP-8): the process retires and recreates sessions and tasks, the developer recreates the cloud environment and Claude's GitHub connection from a page the process writes, and each open job restarts from its branch and pull request (JOB-6). The process is pinned per tranche, changed only by a recorded act, and judged by two numbers neither earlier process measured.
